When a water heater problem is an emergency

Three situations cannot wait for a business-hours call. Water leaking from the tank body, a gas smell near the unit, and a pressure relief valve that is discharging steadily all need a plumber the same day.

A tank that simply runs out of hot water fast is less urgent, but it still tends to fail at the worst hour. We take those calls on the same line, and we usually offer the first open slot.

Before the plumber arrives

  • !Cold water supply. Close the shutoff valve on the cold line above the heater to stop a leak from feeding.
  • !Power or gas. Switch the electric unit off at the breaker, or turn the gas control to pilot or off. Never relight a pilot in a room that smells of gas.
  • !Clear the area. Move stored boxes and chemicals away from the heater and its drain pan.
  • !Do not touch the relief valve. If it is steaming or dripping, leave it. Closing it off can make the tank dangerous.

No hot water: what we check first

With a gas heater, the usual causes are a pilot or thermocouple problem, a dirty burner, or a failed gas control valve. With an electric heater, they are a tripped high-limit switch, a dead upper or lower heating element, or a failed thermostat.

We test before we replace. Metering an element or reading the gas valve takes minutes and spares you from paying for parts the heater never needed.

A tank that makes hot water that fades quickly often has a lower element gone or a thick layer of sediment at the base. Popping or rumbling noises point to the same sediment, which traps steam and overheats the bottom of the tank.

Leaks from a water heater: where they come from

Not every wet spot means a dead tank. A loose supply connection, a dripping relief valve, a weeping drain valve or a failed expansion tank can all leave a puddle that looks like a tank leak.

If the water is coming from a seam at the bottom of the tank itself, the steel liner has rusted through and repair is not possible. We confirm the source with a dry paper towel and a flashlight before we call it.

Worth repairing

  • A failed element or thermostat
  • A bad gas valve or thermocouple
  • A dripping relief valve or drain valve
  • A tank under about ten years old with no leak

Time to replace

  • Water leaking from the tank seam
  • Heavy rust at the base or in the hot water
  • A unit past its expected service life
  • Repeated repairs within one year

Gas, electric and hybrid heaters in Puyallup homes

Most Puyallup homes use a tank-style gas or electric water heater, and a growing number use hybrid heat pump units that pair an electric element with a compressor. Hybrid units add a condensate drain and an air filter that are easy to overlook when a unit stops heating.

If your heater is a tankless unit, a different set of faults applies, such as scale on the heat exchanger and error codes from the control board, and the repair path is different.

Washington water heater installations are expected to be strapped for earthquake safety. A permit tip sheet published by the mybuildingpermit.com program, based on the 2015 Uniform Plumbing Code with Washington amendments, describes straps in the upper and lower thirds of the tank and an expansion tank on a closed system.

Those are installation rules rather than repair rules, but it explains why we look at the straps, the discharge pipe and the expansion tank during every repair visit. Ask the City of Puyallup building department which code edition applies to your unit.

Water heaters in garages near South Hill and downtown

In Puyallup, many water heaters sit in an attached garage or a utility closet. The permit tip sheet cited above notes that in a garage, the ignition source should sit at least 18 inches above the floor unless the heater is rated flammable-vapor resistant, and that a heater in a vehicle path needs a steel bollard.

That means a heater that was moved, re-installed or stands where a car can bump it deserves a code check as part of the repair, not only a part swap.

Codes and references for this page

Water heater repairs follow the manufacturer's instructions and the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as amended by Washington. Items such as the temperature and pressure relief valve and its discharge pipe are safety parts, so they should be replaced rather than capped or plugged.

Washington requires a licensed plumbing contractor for plumbing work done for others under RCW 18.106, and the Department of Labor and Industries lets you check a license at lni.wa.gov/verify.
