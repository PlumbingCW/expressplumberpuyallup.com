Signs of a hidden leak and when it is an emergency

Most leaks announce themselves long before anything drips. The signs below are the ones we hear about most often, and any one of them is worth a call.

The meter keeps moving

Turn off every fixture, then watch the small triangle or dial on your water meter for a minute. If it still turns, water is leaving the system somewhere.

A higher bill with no change in habits

A jump of more than a few dollars with the same household routine is a leak signal, especially when it repeats the next month.

Stains, bubbling paint or a musty smell

A ceiling stain that grows after a shower often points to the drain or supply line above it. A musty smell with no visible water points to a wall cavity.

Warm spots on the floor

A patch of floor that feels warm underfoot can mean a hot-water line is leaking under the slab.

Hissing or low pressure

A hiss with every fixture closed, or pressure that drops at every tap, can mean a leak on the main supply.

Wet ground in the yard

A soft spot, a patch of unusually green lawn or water around the meter box can mean a leak on the buried line.

Water meter running or a high water bill

This is the most common way a leak is discovered. A meter that moves with everything off is a clean test, and a toilet that runs silently can look the same, so we check toilets first by adding a drop of food coloring to the tank and watching the bowl.

If you are a City of Puyallup water customer, your bill and your meter come from the city utility. If you are in a different district, such as a South Hill neighborhood served by another provider, ask that provider where your meter sits and how to read it.

A leak that only runs when an irrigation zone fires, or only when the water heater cycles, will not show every minute. We use a pressure test on the isolated line and, when needed, a logger on the meter to catch an intermittent leak.

How we find it without tearing open the house

Detection starts with isolation. We close sections of the plumbing one at a time, watch the pressure, and narrow the leak to a supply line, a drain line, a hot line or a cold line.

From there, the method depends on what the section is built of and where it runs. The table shows how each tool is used.

Slab leaks and leaks under the house

If your home sits on a concrete slab, a pressurized line running under it can leak for a long time before the floor shows any sign. Warm spots, a constant hiss, higher bills and a meter that moves are the usual clues, and we confirm with listening and thermal tools before any floor is touched.

Once the leak is located, you have options. A localized spot repair through a small opening, a reroute of the line through walls or attic, or replacement of the line are all possible, and the right one depends on the pipe's condition and how many leaks it has already had.

Homes with a crawlspace allow a direct look. Water dripping from a joist, wet insulation, a pooling spot on the soil or an area that smells earthy can all point to a supply or drain leak overhead, and an under-house check usually answers the question in a single visit.

Ceiling, wall and attic leaks

Water travels along framing before it shows, so the stain on a ceiling is rarely directly under the leak. We trace it by moisture readings and by running fixtures above the stain one at a time.

A bathroom above a stain makes the tub drain, shower valve and toilet supply the first suspects. A second-floor leak may also come from a roof or window, and we will tell you if the evidence points outside the plumbing, so you call the right trade.

Buried line, yard and irrigation leaks

Water entering the house passes through a buried service line from the meter. A leak there may show as wet soil, a drop in pressure, or a meter that spins with the house shut off. Locating the exact point lets a repair crew dig a small hole instead of a long trench, which is covered on the water line repair page.

Irrigation systems add their own failure points. A cracked valve, a broken head or a joint that only leaks under pressure can add to a high bill, and a zone-by-zone pressure test finds them.

If you have a pool or spa, we can run a pressure test on the plumbing between the equipment and the water, but we do not repair pool shells or liners.

Winter wet and what it hides in Puyallup

Most of Puyallup's rain falls between October and March, and a saturated lawn can hide a buried-line leak because wet ground is already expected. If your yard stays soft in a dry spell, or your meter moves with everything off, call before the wet season masks the symptoms.

After heavy-rain events, such as the December 2025 flooding that led to evacuation notices in Puyallup and Sumner, check your basement, crawlspace and meter box for standing water and tell your plumber what you saw.

Codes and references for this page

Leak detection is a diagnostic service. Repairs that follow, such as replacing a supply line or opening a wall, fall under the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as amended by Washington and may need a permit. Document the damage with photos for your insurer, since policies differ on what they cover.

Washington requires a licensed plumbing contractor for plumbing work done for others under RCW 18.106, and the Department of Labor and Industries lets you check a license at lni.wa.gov/verify.
