After-hours plumbing emergencies in commercial buildings

A commercial property carries more fixtures and more consequences. A toilet that will not flush in a one-bathroom shop can close the doors, and a floor drain backup in a restaurant kitchen is a health issue as well as a plumbing issue.

Our dispatch line is open 24 hours a day, every day. When you call, tell us the type of business, where water is coming from, and whether you can reach the main shutoff.

Before we arrive

  • !Close the main valve. Know where the building's main and the branch shutoffs are, and mark them for staff.
  • !Protect the area. Move stock, electronics and records off the floor, and keep customers out of wet zones.
  • !Stop using affected fixtures. Post signs on restrooms and sinks that are backing up.
  • !Call your property manager. If you rent, notify the landlord so building-wide shutoffs and access are arranged.

Common commercial calls

Main drain backups

Several fixtures slow together or a floor drain rises. See sewer line cleaning for how mains are cleared.

Kitchen and grease lines

Fat, oil and grease coat pipes and traps. A schedule of cleaning prevents emergency backups.

Restroom failures

Running toilets, leaking flush valves and clogged urinals are the most common calls, and fixtures run constantly.

Water heater failures

Commercial tank and tankless units follow the same failure patterns as residential ones but at higher demand.

Leaks above ceilings

Supply or drain leaks above a suspended ceiling can cause damage before staff notice.

Water pressure changes

Sudden low pressure can mean a valve, a pressure reducing valve failure or a leak on the service line.

Commercial leak detection

Hidden leaks in commercial buildings are found by isolating zones and listening, pressure testing and thermal imaging, the same methods described on the leak detection page. Large buildings take longer and often need access to ceilings and chases. We are honest about what we can find on a first visit, and we recommend a plan in steps rather than promising a quick result on a complex building.

Grease trap cleaning and kitchen lines

A grease trap or interceptor catches fats, oils and grease before they enter the sewer. When it fills, grease passes through and builds up downstream, and the first sign is often a slow floor drain or a bad smell.

We clean traps and the lines around them, and recommend how often to service them based on what comes out. For lines that keep coating, a hydro jetting pass cleans the pipe wall and a camera confirms the result.

Backflow testing and prevention

A backflow preventer stops water from reversing direction and contaminating the supply. Commercial buildings, irrigation systems, fire lines and some food-service equipment may require one, and the assembly usually needs periodic testing by a certified tester.

Requirements depend on the type of building, the hazard and the water provider, so ask your provider which assemblies at your address require testing and on what schedule. Backflow testing is a planned service, not an emergency, so it is scheduled on weekdays.

Storm drain and parking lot drain cleaning

Catch basins, parking lot drains and roof drains fill with silt, leaves and trash, and in a wet season they can flood a lot or a loading dock. We clear them with a cable or jetter and check that they drain to their outlet. For larger cleaning jobs on municipal or large-property systems, a specialty vacuum contractor may be better suited, and we will tell you.

Codes and references for this page

Commercial plumbing follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as amended by Washington, and some work, such as backflow assemblies, grease interceptors and fixture counts, adds local rules. Ask the City of Puyallup or Pierce County which permits apply to your building.

Washington requires a licensed plumbing contractor for plumbing work done for others under RCW 18.106, and the Department of Labor and Industries lets you check a license at lni.wa.gov/verify.
