Shower Plumbing Rough-In for Puyallup Homes: What to Know Before the Wall Closes

Shower plumbing rough-in is the work that happens inside the wall before the shower is finished. The valve, the supply lines, the drain and the blocking are all set and tested, and once the tile is up, they are locked in.

Whether you are remodeling a bathroom in an older Puyallup house or finishing a South Hill basement, getting the rough-in right is cheaper than fixing it. This guide covers what it includes, the details to confirm, and who is allowed to do the work in Washington.

Written by the Puyallup Express Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

What shower rough-in includes

Rough-in is a set of connected pieces. A mixing valve sits behind the wall at a chosen height. Supply lines carry hot and cold water to that valve, and a pipe carries the mixed water to the showerhead. A tub spout, if there is one, gets its own line. The drain runs from the shower floor to the building's drain system, with a trap beneath.

Blocking, which is solid wood between studs, supports grab bars, the showerhead arm and heavy fixtures. Waterproofing, such as a membrane or backer board, is a separate step done by the tile setter.

  1. 1

    Plan the layout

    Decide the shower size, valve location, showerhead height and any niche, then confirm the valve brand and its depth requirements.

  2. 2

    Rough in the supply

    Run hot and cold to the valve, install the valve, and run the outlet to the showerhead.

  3. 3

    Rough in the drain

    Set the drain at the correct location, size and slope, connect it to the system and vent the trap.

  4. 4

    Pressure and leak test

    Cap the lines, pressurize them and watch the gauge. Fill the shower pan and check that it holds water before tile.

  5. 5

    Inspect and close

    If a permit applies, schedule the inspection before the wall is covered.

Details that are easy to get wrong

Valve depth

A valve set too deep or too shallow in the wall cannot be fitted with its trim. Most valves have a depth range printed on the body.

Valve height

A common height places the valve handle at a comfortable reach from outside the spray. Tub-shower combos use a different height than showers alone.

Drain location and slope

A drain off center makes a floor pitch awkward. The drain also needs enough slope to move water, and a vent so it drains smoothly.

Blocking

Missing blocking means a loose showerhead arm or a grab bar that pulls out of the wall.

Mixing valve type

Pressure-balance valves keep temperature steady when other fixtures run. Thermostatic valves let you set an exact temperature. Which one suits you depends on the household.

Why testing before tile matters

A leak behind a finished shower wall is expensive, because the tile, the backer and sometimes the framing must come out. A pressure test on the supply lines and a water test on the shower pan find a leak while the wall is still open.

Most plumbers hold the test for at least a short period and leave the gauge for the inspector to see. If your plumber skips it, ask why.

Tub-shower combinations and walk-in showers

A tub-shower combination uses one valve for both the tub spout and the showerhead, with a diverter that sends water to one or the other. The tub drain, overflow and trap sit under the tub, so you need access from below or a panel in the adjoining room.

A walk-in shower adds a floor drain, a pan that slopes toward it, and a curb or a curbless entry. Curbless entries need the floor framing lowered, which makes planning with the builder even more important. Ask for the drain location and the floor slope to be settled before anything is cut.

Permits and licensing for a Puyallup shower project

The City of Puyallup runs an online permit portal and City Hall is at 333 S. Meridian. Permit rules vary by the work and the jurisdiction, so ask the City, or Pierce County if you are in unincorporated South Hill, what applies to a shower rough-in at your address.

Washington's Department of Labor and Industries states that, since July 1, 2021, all plumbing contract work must be performed by a licensed plumbing contractor under chapter 18.106 RCW. You can check any plumber's license at lni.wa.gov/verify before you hire.
