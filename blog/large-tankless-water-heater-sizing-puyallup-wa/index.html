Sizing a Large Tankless Water Heater for a Puyallup, WA Home

People who ask about a large or 75-gallon class tankless water heater are usually describing a big household that wants endless hot water. Tankless units are not sized in gallons. They are sized in flow rate, how many gallons per minute they can heat, and in how much they can raise the temperature of the incoming water.

Getting that sizing right is the difference between a unit that delivers and one that runs lukewarm during the morning rush. This guide shows how to size one for a Puyallup home.

Written by the Puyallup Express Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

Why gallons do not apply

A tank water heater stores hot water, so its size in gallons is the buffer you draw from. A tankless unit stores almost nothing and heats as the water flows. What matters is how much water per minute it can bring to the temperature you want.

When someone asks for a 75-gallon tankless, they usually mean the capacity of a big tank replaced by a unit that never runs out. The right match is a unit sized to the household's peak demand.

The sizing steps

  1. 1

    List your hot fixtures

    Showers, kitchen sink, dishwasher, washer, tub and any soaking or body-spray fixtures.

  2. 2

    Add up peak flow

    Estimate gallons per minute for the fixtures that can run at the same time.

  3. 3

    Find the temperature rise

    Subtract the coldest incoming water temperature from the temperature you want at the tap.

  4. 4

    Match the unit

    Choose a unit whose rated flow at that rise meets or exceeds your peak demand.

  5. 5

    Check the gas and venting

    Make sure the gas line, vent and electrical support the unit.

These are rough values, and the labels on your fixtures give the real numbers. A big house with three showers running at once is a different problem from a small house with one.

Winter sets the size

Incoming water is coldest in winter, so the temperature rise is largest and the same unit delivers fewer gallons per minute. Size for January, not August.

A unit that is just big enough in summer will run out in winter. A plumber should size from your coldest realistic incoming temperature and the highest simultaneous demand.

A large tankless suits

  • A big household with staggered use
  • Homes with room for a larger gas line
  • People who want to free up floor space
  • Owners ready to flush annually

A tank or a pair of units suits

  • Households with many simultaneous showers
  • Homes where the gas line cannot be upgraded
  • Very tight budgets
  • Properties where hot water is rarely used all at once

Some very large homes use two tankless units linked together, or a tankless unit with a small buffer tank. These setups spread the load and give a backup if one unit fails.

A hybrid heat pump water heater is another route for big households that want efficiency without upgrading the gas line, though it needs space and air around it.

Before you order a large unit

  • !Check the gas line. A big unit can need a larger line and meter capacity.
  • !Plan the vent route. Longer vents lose capacity.
  • !Ask about the permit. Gas and panel work often needs one.
  • !Plan for maintenance. Isolation valves make annual flushing simple.

Recirculation and big households

Large households also complain about waiting for hot water, and a bigger tankless unit does not shorten that wait. A recirculation pump or a small buffer tank keeps hot water close to the taps.

Ask about a recirculation loop when you plan the install. It is cheaper to add the return line and the pump while the plumbing is open than later.

A quick sanity check at home

Run your busiest realistic morning in your head. Count which showers, the dishwasher and the washer might run together, and add their flows. If the total is higher than a single unit's rated flow at your winter rise, plan for two units or a different approach.

Bring that list to the plumber. It makes the walk-through quicker and the recommendation better.

Cold water and code in Puyallup

Western Washington has wet, mild winters, and the incoming water temperature in cold months drives the temperature rise your unit must handle. Ask your installer what winter inlet temperature they used.

A permit tip sheet from the mybuildingpermit.com program lists code points for water heaters, based on the 2015 Uniform Plumbing Code with Washington amendments, including seismic strapping for tanks and relief valve discharge. Confirm with the City of Puyallup or Pierce County which rules apply to your address.
