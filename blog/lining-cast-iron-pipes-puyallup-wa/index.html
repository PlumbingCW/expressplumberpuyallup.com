Lining Cast Iron Pipes in Puyallup, WA: When It Works and When It Does Not

Cast iron pipes do not fail overnight. They scale from the inside, crack at the joints and eventually leak or clog, and in an older home the repair can mean tearing up a floor or a yard. Pipe lining offers another path, in which a new pipe is formed inside the old one without digging along its length.

It is not right for every pipe, and a camera has to prove that before anyone commits. This guide explains how lining works, what it can and cannot fix, and how to decide.

Written by the Puyallup Express Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

Why cast iron pipe wears out

Cast iron was used for drain and sewer pipe for much of the last century because it is strong and quiet. Its weakness is corrosion from the inside. Wastewater is slightly acidic, and over decades it eats at the pipe wall and builds a rough layer of rust and scale called tuberculation.

That rough layer catches debris, narrows the opening and makes clogs more frequent. Eventually a section thins, cracks or leaks at a joint, and sewage seeps into the soil or the crawlspace.

How pipe lining works

  1. 1

    Clean the pipe

    Cable or hydro jet the line to remove scale, roots and debris so the new liner can bond.

  2. 2

    Camera inspection

    Record the pipe's condition, find offsets, collapses and branch connections.

  3. 3

    Insert the liner

    A flexible sleeve soaked in resin is pulled or inverted into the pipe.

  4. 4

    Cure

    The resin hardens with heat, steam or light, forming a smooth new pipe inside the old one.

  5. 5

    Reopen and inspect

    Cut open the branch connections, run the camera again and confirm a smooth, continuous lining.

Nothing needs to be dug along the pipe's length. Access is through existing cleanouts or small openings at each end.

When lining works

Lining often suits

  • Cracks or small holes in an otherwise round pipe
  • Root intrusion at joints
  • Scale and pitting along a long run
  • Pipes under driveways, slabs or landscaping

Lining does not suit

  • A collapsed or crushed pipe
  • A large offset or a deep belly that holds water
  • A pipe with many sharp bends
  • A pipe that is too damaged to hold the liner

Lining reduces the inside diameter of the pipe slightly, but because the new surface is smooth, flow is often as good as before or better. It does not fix a sagging pipe, because a liner follows the shape of what it lines.

If the grade is wrong, water will still pool in the low spot. That is why the camera pass comes before any decision.

Questions to ask before you agree

What did the camera show?

Ask to see the footage of the problem areas and the joints.

Why lining and not replacement?

A good answer references the pipe's condition and the surface above it.

What is the warranty on the liner?

Ask what the warranty covers and how long it lasts.

How are branches reconnected?

Each connection must be cut and checked on camera.

Is a permit needed?

Rules vary, so ask the City of Puyallup or Pierce County.

What to expect on the day

A lining job usually takes a day or two. The crew cleans and cameras the line, installs the liner, cures it and reopens the branches, with a final camera pass to confirm. During that time you will not be able to use the affected drains, so plan around laundry and showers.

Ask for the before and after footage, the length lined and the warranty in writing. The video is also useful when you sell the house.

Older pipe in Puyallup

Puyallup was platted by Ezra Meeker in 1877 and incorporated in 1890, so the area near downtown and the Meeker Mansion has some of the oldest housing in the city. Homes built before the mid-1900s are the ones most likely to have original cast iron or clay drains.

South Hill, an unincorporated area that grew 143.9 percent between 1990 and 2000, has far more recent housing, where plastic pipe is more common. That does not mean newer homes are free of drain trouble, but cast iron lining is mostly an older-home question.
