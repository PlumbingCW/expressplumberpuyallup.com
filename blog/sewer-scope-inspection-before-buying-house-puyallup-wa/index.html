Sewer Scope Inspection Before Buying a House in Puyallup, WA

A standard home inspection looks at the house above ground. The sewer line, which runs underground from the house to the street, is usually outside its scope. A sewer scope inspection puts a camera in that line and shows what is actually in there.

If you are buying a house in Puyallup or South Hill, it is one of the few inexpensive checks that can reveal a costly problem before you own it. This guide explains what the scope shows, when to schedule it and what to do with the results.

Written by the Puyallup Express Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

What a sewer scope shows

A plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible rod through a cleanout and watches the pipe on a monitor. The camera records the pipe material, the joints, any roots, scale or grease, cracks or breaks, sags that hold water, and the distance to each problem.

A locator on the surface marks the depth and position of the line, which is useful for any future repair and for planning landscaping.

What you might see and what it means
FindingWhat it meansLikely next step
Clean, smooth pipeNo visible defectsNo action, keep the footage for your records
Roots at jointsRoots are entering, usually where pipe sections joinCut and jet now, then plan a lining or repair
Scale or heavy greasePipe narrowed by buildupJetting, then re-camera
Cracks or an offset jointPipe is damagedGet a repair quote and negotiate
Sag or belly with standing waterPipe has settledReplacement of that section
Collapse or a blockage the camera cannot passSerious damageTreat as a major cost before closing

When to schedule it

Book the scope as soon as your offer is accepted, during the inspection or due diligence window. Results that arrive after the window closes give you little leverage.

Ask your agent about the contract terms for sewer inspection, and whether the seller must provide access to the cleanout. If the house has no cleanout, a plumber may need to pull a toilet to get in, and that should be agreed with the seller.

How to prepare

  • !Ask for access. Confirm that the plumber can reach the cleanout or a toilet.
  • !Ask for the footage. Get the video and the notes in writing.
  • !Be present or reachable. A plumber can explain findings the moment they see them.
  • !Do not skip older homes or new ones. New construction can have defects too.

How to use the results

A clean scope gives you confidence. A scope with defects gives you a basis to ask the seller for a repair, a credit or a price reduction, or to walk away if the problem is severe. Pair the footage with a written quote from a plumber so the number is real.

If you decide to proceed with a flawed line, plan for it. A rooted line can be cut and jetted now and lined later, and a damaged section can be repaired before it fails.

What a scope cannot tell you

The scope shows

  • The pipe from the house to where the camera reaches
  • Pipe material and condition
  • Roots, scale and grease
  • Cracks, offsets and sags

The scope does not show

  • The city's main beyond the property line
  • The condition of lines it cannot enter
  • Future damage
  • Private lines on another lot

What affects the cost of a sewer scope

The price is mostly the plumber's time and access.

AccessA cleanout is simple, while pulling a toilet takes more time.
Line lengthA long run from a back house takes longer.
Pre-cleaningA blocked line may need cleaning before the camera can pass.
LocatingMarking depth and position is a separate step.
Report and footageWritten notes and video for the file may be extra.

  3. 3

    Review the footage

  4. 4

    Get a repair quote

    If there is a problem, ask for a written scope and number.

  5. 5

    Negotiate or decide

    Use the quote to ask for a repair, a credit or a price change, or to withdraw within your contingency.

Doing this early leaves time to respond. A plumber's schedule can fill up when the window is short, so call as soon as the offer is accepted.

Warning signs you can spot before the scope

Look for slow drains, gurgling when a toilet flushes, a floor drain with an odor, lush green patches or sunken ground in the yard, and large mature trees near the sewer path. Ask the seller about past backups and repairs.

None of these prove a problem, but each is a reason to schedule the scope and a reason to watch the footage closely.

Puyallup was incorporated in 1890 and its downtown blocks include some of the oldest housing in the area. South Hill, an unincorporated area, grew 143.9 percent between 1990 and 2000, so many of its homes are newer. The right scope question depends on which you are buying.
