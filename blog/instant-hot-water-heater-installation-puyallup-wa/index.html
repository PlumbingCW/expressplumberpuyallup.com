Installing an Instant Hot Water Heater in Puyallup, WA: What It Takes

An instant hot water heater, usually a tankless or on-demand unit, heats water as it flows instead of storing it. People ask for one because they want endless hot water, a smaller footprint or lower standby losses. The word instant is a little generous, since water still has to travel from the unit to the faucet.

This guide explains the types, what the installation requires in a typical Puyallup home and the questions worth asking before you buy.

A recirculation pump is not a tankless heater, but it solves the problem people mean when they say instant. Hot water is kept moving through the pipes, so it arrives at the tap quickly.

What changes when you install a tankless unit

Gas supply

A tankless gas unit burns far more gas at once than a tank. The existing gas line may be too small, and the meter may need to be considered.

Venting

Tankless units need their own vent, often sealed stainless or plastic, and the route through the wall or roof matters.

Electrical

Gas units need a small power supply. Electric units need large circuits, and the panel may need an upgrade.

Water lines

The inlet and outlet connections change, and isolation valves are added for flushing.

Condensate

Condensing models produce acidic water that must drain somewhere safe.

Space and clearances

Each unit has clearance rules from combustibles and from windows or openings.

Because of these changes, a conversion is a planned project. We start with a visit to measure the gas line, check the panel, find a vent route and size the unit by the number of fixtures you run at once.

The sizing is by flow rate in gallons per minute and the temperature rise needed. Cold incoming water in winter needs more heating than in summer, so the unit must be sized for the coldest months.

Seismic and code items

A permit tip sheet from the mybuildingpermit.com program is based on the 2015 Uniform Plumbing Code with Washington amendments. It lists items for water heaters, including a shutoff valve on the incoming cold line, a gas shutoff and sediment trap on gas units, and venting to the manufacturer's specification. Which code edition applies to your address is a question for the City of Puyallup building department, or Pierce County in unincorporated areas.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • !What is the flow rate at my winter temperature rise? A unit that works in August may disappoint in January.
  • !Does my gas line and panel support it? If not, ask what the upgrade costs before comparing prices.
  • !Where does the vent go? Look at the exact route and the termination.
  • !Who handles flushing? Make sure isolation valves are installed.

Is it worth switching

Tankless units suit homes that want endless hot water, have wall space and can handle the gas or electrical upgrade. They do not always save money. Savings depend on your use, and the upfront cost is higher than a tank.

If your main complaint is waiting at a far faucet, a recirculation pump or a small point-of-use unit may solve it for less. We can look at the layout and tell you which option fits.

Point-of-use heaters for a faraway fixture

A small electric unit under a far bathroom or kitchen sink can cut the wait for hot water at that single tap. It works well for an addition, a laundry room or a detached office where running a new line from the main heater is costly.

The tradeoff is capacity. A mini unit serves one or two fixtures, so it is a fix for a distance problem, not a replacement for a whole-house heater. Check the circuit it needs before you buy.

What Puyallup's water means for an on-demand unit

The City of Puyallup's 2024 water quality report lists average hardness at 89 ppm and says all sources are treated with chlorine. That is moderate hardness, so scale will form in a tankless exchanger over time, and annual flushing is a sensible plan from the start.

If you are on another water provider, ask for its report before you buy, because hardness and iron levels change what maintenance a tankless unit needs.
